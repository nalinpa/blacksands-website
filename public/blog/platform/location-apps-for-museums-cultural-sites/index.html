How Museums and Cultural Sites Use Location Apps

Museums and cultural sites use location apps to guide visitors through a space, layer in deeper content for those who want it, offer multiple languages, and extend the experience beyond their own walls into the surrounding area.

How do cultural sites use location apps?

As a flexible, self-paced layer over the physical visit. A location app can lead people through a museum or a heritage site at their own speed, surfacing the right content at each exhibit or spot, going as deep as the visitor wants, and speaking their language. It does what a printed guide and a fixed audio handset do, but with more depth, easier updates and no hardware to hand out and sanitise.

Guiding and interpreting

The core use is wayfinding and interpretation combined. Visitors follow a self-paced route through the collection or site, with each stop unlocking the story behind what they are looking at. People who want the highlights get a short path, while those who want detail can dig in. The result is a visit that suits both the rushed and the curious, without the site having to print for every level of interest.

Going deeper, in any language

An app removes the limits of print and signage. Optional layers let visitors choose how much they want, from a one-line label to a long backstory, without cluttering the physical space. And because the content is digital, offering it in several languages is a matter of translation, not reprinting and re-mounting everything. For sites with international visitors, that multilingual reach is often the single biggest draw.

Beyond the walls

Location apps also let a site extend past its own building. A museum can connect to related sites across a town, or build a trail that ties a cluster of heritage places into one journey, guiding visitors between them with the same app. That turns a single indoor visit into a wider experience of a place, and links cultural organisations that might otherwise sit as separate, unconnected stops on a map.
